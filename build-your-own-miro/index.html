How to Vibe Code Your Own Miro Alternative

Sticky notes, voting dots, and one infinite-ish canvas. Miro charges $10–$20 per person per month — that’s $120–$240 a year per person — for something you can replace with focused software of your own. Here is the honest scope, the honest timeline, and the exact prompt to hand your coding agent.

from 12 hoursFor workshops, absolutely

Who this replacement is for

This build targets a facilitator running remote retrospectives and planning workshops. The goal: let a group add, arrange, group, and vote on sticky notes in real time. If you need more than that, keep paying — the point of building it yourself is owning a tool shaped exactly like your workflow, not re-implementing a venture-funded roadmap.

Same build, different logo: this v1 also replaces Mural, FigJam and Lucidspark — they all solve the same core job.

How long it actually takes

One number would be a lie, so here are three. Each tier is a real, usable product — pick the one that matches how much of Miro you actually use.

EstimateWhat you get
12 hourscanvas, sticky notes, pan and zoom
1 weeklive cursors, dot voting, frames, exports
3 months+freehand drawing, diagrams, hundreds of simultaneous editors — the part you should probably skip

What a minimal Miro alternative needs

  • pan and zoom canvas with movable colored notes
  • named participants and visible cursors
  • timer, anonymous mode, and dot voting
  • frames for workshop sections and facilitator lock controls
  • export the finished board as PNG or structured JSON

Data model

Board, Participant, Frame, Note, Vote

Integrations

WebSocket rooms

Capability context

Astro, Alpine, Durable Objects

The guardrail

Validate coordinates and permissions server-side and cap board object counts.

Deliberate non-goals

Do not support arbitrary drawing, diagrams, embeds, templates marketplace, or hundreds of simultaneous editors.

The complete build prompt

Copy this into your coding agent of choice. It is scoped for a useful v1 — journeys, screens, business rules, data model, security, tests, and acceptance scenarios included. Pick your stack:

You are building a production-ready software product named “Stickywall”, a deliberately focused alternative to Miro. Build a complete, usable vertical slice—not a landing page, static mockup, or disconnected collection of components.

WORKING AGREEMENT
Before writing implementation code, produce a short technical plan that names the routes or pages, server actions or endpoints, data tables, important state transitions, authorization boundaries, background jobs, and external adapters. Resolve contradictions in favor of the narrow audience and non-goals below. Prefer a small, legible architecture over speculative abstraction, but do not omit persistence, validation, error handling, or tests.

PRODUCT BRIEF
Primary user: a facilitator running remote retrospectives and planning workshops.
Primary outcome: let a group add, arrange, group, and vote on sticky notes in real time.
Product principle: optimize the exact workflow below instead of copying the full breadth of Miro. A first-time user should understand what to do from the interface itself, without a tour or documentation.

END-TO-END USER JOURNEYS
Implement all of these flows through the real interface and persistent data layer:
1. A facilitator creates a board, adds named frames for retrospective sections, chooses note colors, enables anonymous contributions, and shares a participant link.
2. Participants join with display names, pan to a frame, add and edit notes, move related notes into clusters, and see other cursors and updates in real time.
3. The facilitator starts a timer, locks note editing, opens a three-dot voting round, reveals results, then exports the finished board as PNG and JSON.

SCREENS AND INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE
Build these as coherent responsive views. Each screen must specify its primary action, secondary actions, visible status, validation feedback, empty state, loading or pending state, success confirmation, and recoverable failure state.
1. Board lobby and setup: board title, participant link, anonymous mode, frame list, object counts, facilitator permissions, and open-board action.
2. Collaborative canvas: bounded pan and zoom, frames, movable colored notes, selection outlines, participant cursors, connection state, and mini-map or reset-view control.
3. Facilitation panel: participant roster, timer, anonymous-mode status, editing locks, voting budget, start and reveal controls, and frame navigation.
4. Results and export: vote totals by note and frame, tie visibility, PNG capture preview, structured JSON download, and board-lock or reopen action.

CORE CAPABILITIES
1. pan and zoom canvas with movable colored notes
2. named participants and visible cursors
3. timer, anonymous mode, and dot voting
4. frames for workshop sections and facilitator lock controls
5. export the finished board as PNG or structured JSON

DETAILED BEHAVIOR AND BUSINESS RULES
Treat these as server-enforced product requirements, not interface suggestions:
1. Validate note text length, color, frame membership, coordinates, dimensions, role, and board object limits server-side before broadcasting a mutation.
2. Assign every accepted mutation a monotonically ordered board revision so reconnecting clients can replay missed changes and resolve stale moves predictably.
3. Anonymous mode hides authors from participants but retains an internal participant identifier for ownership, rate limiting, and facilitator moderation.
4. During voting, enforce one active round and a fixed per-participant budget; votes remain hidden until reveal and cannot target deleted notes.

DATA MODEL AND LIFECYCLE
Design a small relational schema centered on Board, Participant, Frame, Note, Vote. Before implementing it, document:
1. Each table’s purpose, primary key, ownership or tenant boundary, timestamps, status fields, and important attributes.
2. Foreign keys, uniqueness constraints, check constraints, indexes needed by the named screens, and transaction boundaries for multi-record changes.
3. The allowed lifecycle or state transitions, who may trigger each transition, which transitions are terminal or reversible, and what audit history must remain immutable.
4. Archive, retention, and deletion behavior, including what happens to dependent records and external files.
5. Idempotency strategy for submissions, jobs, imports, notifications, webhooks, or retries where applicable.
Use migrations rather than ad-hoc schema creation. Store time instants consistently and retain named timezone context whenever local schedules or dates matter. Never rely on a counter, disabled button, or client-side check to preserve a business invariant.

USERS, AUTHENTICATION, AND PERMISSIONS
Implement only the roles required by the stated audience. Make the ownership and visibility model explicit before coding. Enforce authorization in every server-side query and mutation, including search, exports, attachments, live updates, and guessed URLs—not merely by hiding controls. Use secure session defaults, protect state-changing requests, and provide an understandable signed-out, expired-session, and forbidden state. Seed distinct users when multiple roles are required so permissions can be demonstrated and tested.

INTERACTION AND VISUAL DIRECTION
The product should feel fast, calm, focused, and credible rather than like a generic admin template. Use a clear visual hierarchy, restrained color, readable typography, generous hit targets, and consistent placement for primary actions. Start with server-rendered HTML and progressively enhance only the interactions that benefit from it. The core workflow must remain understandable if enhancement fails.

Start with server-rendered Astro pages and ordinary HTML forms. Use HTMX for form submissions, partial navigation, and server-driven updates, then Alpine.js only for small local browser state. The core workflow must remain understandable if either enhancement layer fails.

Design mobile layouts intentionally instead of simply stacking desktop panels. Support keyboard navigation, visible focus, semantic landmarks, explicit labels, useful page titles, reduced-motion preferences, and screen-reader announcements for asynchronous results. Never use color alone to communicate state. Destructive actions require clear scope and confirmation; safe repeated actions should be idempotent.

TECHNICAL DIRECTION
Build this version with the AHA stack: Astro for routing, layouts, and server-rendered pages; HTMX for interactions that benefit from HTML fragment responses; and Alpine.js for small, local interface state. Prefer Cloudflare D1 for relational persistence, R2 for object storage, Workers for server endpoints and scheduled work, Durable Objects only for coordinated real-time state, and Workflows or Queues for durable background jobs—but only when the product requirements call for them.

Keep domain rules in testable server-side modules instead of route handlers or UI components. Separate persistence, external providers, and background work behind small interfaces without building a framework. Prefer ordinary HTML forms and URLs for durable navigation; use optimistic interaction only when failure can be reconciled clearly.

The product brief currently identifies Astro, Alpine, Durable Objects as capability context. Preserve any required native, browser-only, edge, storage, real-time, or background-processing capability through a narrow adapter appropriate to the selected framework. If the core workflow genuinely requires native or browser APIs, keep that runtime as the primary execution surface rather than simulating inaccessible capabilities or inventing an unnecessary web surface.

Integrate with WebSocket rooms. For every integration:
- List required environment variables in an .env.example without real secrets.
- Add a small adapter with timeouts, normalized errors, and a deterministic local fake or development path.
- Verify inbound signatures and deduplicate provider events where supported.
- Keep credentials server-side, encrypt long-lived provider tokens at rest, and redact secrets and sensitive payloads from logs.
- Define retry, backoff, and idempotency behavior for any side effect that can be repeated.

SECURITY AND PRIVACY
Validate coordinates and permissions server-side and cap board object counts.
Validate, normalize, and length-limit all untrusted input on the server. Escape rendered content by default, sanitize any intentionally accepted markup, rate-limit public or abuse-prone actions, and use private object storage plus short-lived authorized URLs for sensitive files. Collect the minimum personal data necessary for the named workflow. Document retention and deletion behavior. Add specific protections for the riskier surfaces in this app, such as uploads, redirects, outbound requests, email delivery, OAuth, webhooks, CSV import or export, and real-time connections.

ACCEPTANCE SCENARIOS
Automate these app-specific scenarios at the most appropriate level:
1. Given two participants move the same note nearly simultaneously, when both updates reach the room, every client converges on the server-accepted final position.
2. Given a three-dot voting round, when a participant attempts a fourth vote from another browser request, the server rejects it and totals remain unchanged.
3. Given a participant reconnects after missing several edits, when they provide their last revision, the board replays the gap without duplicating notes or votes.

TESTING
Add focused unit tests for state transitions, authorization predicates, normalization, date or money calculations, and other risky domain rules. Add integration tests for persistence constraints and each external adapter’s success, timeout, retry, and rejection paths. Add at least one browser-level test for every end-to-end journey above, including one small-screen viewport. Tests must use isolated data and run through a documented single command.

OPERATIONS AND FAILURE RECOVERY
Add structured server logs with request, job, or event correlation IDs but no secrets or unnecessarily sensitive data. Make failures actionable in both the interface and logs. Background work must expose pending, succeeded, failed, and retrying states where relevant; do not silently swallow errors. Include safe database migration and rollback guidance, seed data, backup and restore notes, external-data cleanup behavior, and a basic health or diagnostic path appropriate to the stack.

DELIVERABLES
Ship the working application, migrations, representative seed data, tests, .env.example, and a concise README. The README must cover prerequisites, local setup, environment variables, migrations, seed and test commands, deployment, integration setup, backup and restore, security decisions, and known limitations. Seed data should exercise the happy path plus at least one empty, failed, overdue, expired, archived, or permission-restricted state relevant to the product.

DEFINITION OF DONE
The app is complete when a fresh developer can follow the README, create and migrate the database, run the app, sign in as each relevant role, complete every named journey using real persisted data, refresh without losing state, recover from common failures, and use the core interface on phone and desktop. All acceptance scenarios pass, permission boundaries are covered by tests, and no core screen is left as a placeholder.

NON-GOALS
Do not support arbitrary drawing, diagrams, embeds, templates marketplace, or hundreds of simultaneous editors.

END-TO-END USER JOURNEYS
Implement all of these flows through the real interface and persistent data layer:
1. A facilitator creates a board, adds named frames for retrospective sections, chooses note colors, enables anonymous contributions, and shares a participant link.
2. Participants join with display names, pan to a frame, add and edit notes, move related notes into clusters, and see other cursors and updates in real time.
3. The facilitator starts a timer, locks note editing, opens a three-dot voting round, reveals results, then exports the finished board as PNG and JSON.

Build these as coherent responsive views. Each screen must specify its primary action, secondary actions, visible status, validation feedback, empty state, loading or pending state, success confirmation, and recoverable failure state.
1. Board lobby and setup: board title, participant link, anonymous mode, frame list, object counts, facilitator permissions, and open-board action.
2. Collaborative canvas: bounded pan and zoom, frames, movable colored notes, selection outlines, participant cursors, connection state, and mini-map or reset-view control.
3. Facilitation panel: participant roster, timer, anonymous-mode status, editing locks, voting budget, start and reveal controls, and frame navigation.
4. Results and export: vote totals by note and frame, tie visibility, PNG capture preview, structured JSON download, and board-lock or reopen action.

DETAILED BEHAVIOR AND BUSINESS RULES
Treat these as server-enforced product requirements, not interface suggestions:
1. Validate note text length, color, frame membership, coordinates, dimensions, role, and board object limits server-side before broadcasting a mutation.
2. Assign every accepted mutation a monotonically ordered board revision so reconnecting clients can replay missed changes and resolve stale moves predictably.
3. Anonymous mode hides authors from participants but retains an internal participant identifier for ownership, rate limiting, and facilitator moderation.
4. During voting, enforce one active round and a fixed per-participant budget; votes remain hidden until reveal and cannot target deleted notes.

DATA MODEL AND LIFECYCLE
Design a small relational schema centered on Board, Participant, Frame, Note, Vote. Before implementing it, document:
1. Each table’s purpose, primary key, ownership or tenant boundary, timestamps, status fields, and important attributes.
2. Foreign keys, uniqueness constraints, check constraints, indexes needed by the named screens, and transaction boundaries for multi-record changes.
3. The allowed lifecycle or state transitions, who may trigger each transition, which transitions are terminal or reversible, and what audit history must remain immutable.
4. Archive, retention, and deletion behavior, including what happens to dependent records and external files.
5. Idempotency strategy for submissions, jobs, imports, notifications, webhooks, or retries where applicable.
Use migrations rather than ad-hoc schema creation. Store time instants consistently and retain named timezone context whenever local schedules or dates matter. Never rely on a counter, disabled button, or client-side check to preserve a business invariant.

USERS, AUTHENTICATION, AND PERMISSIONS
Implement only the roles required by the stated audience. Make the ownership and visibility model explicit before coding. Enforce authorization in every server-side query and mutation, including search, exports, attachments, live updates, and guessed URLs—not merely by hiding controls. Use secure session defaults, protect state-changing requests, and provide an understandable signed-out, expired-session, and forbidden state. Seed distinct users when multiple roles are required so permissions can be demonstrated and tested.

INTERACTION AND VISUAL DIRECTION
The product should feel fast, calm, focused, and credible rather than like a generic admin template. Use a clear visual hierarchy, restrained color, readable typography, generous hit targets, and consistent placement for primary actions. Start with server-rendered HTML and progressively enhance only the interactions that benefit from it. The core workflow must remain understandable if enhancement fails.

Start with server-rendered Rails views and ordinary forms. Use Turbo for navigation, submissions, and server-driven updates, then Stimulus only for small browser-side behavior. Do not turn the product into a client-side SPA, and keep the core workflow usable when enhancement fails.

Design mobile layouts intentionally instead of simply stacking desktop panels. Support keyboard navigation, visible focus, semantic landmarks, explicit labels, useful page titles, reduced-motion preferences, and screen-reader announcements for asynchronous results. Never use color alone to communicate state. Destructive actions require clear scope and confirmation; safe repeated actions should be idempotent.

TECHNICAL DIRECTION
Build this version with Ruby on Rails and Hotwire: Turbo for navigation, form submissions, and server-driven updates, and Stimulus for small browser-side behavior. Use RESTful controllers with strong parameters and explicit authorization, with domain transitions in testable models or small application services. Use Active Record migrations plus database-level constraints and transactions; Active Storage with an S3-compatible service for private files; Active Job with a durable queue backend for retryable work; Action Mailer for email; and Action Cable only when live updates are required.

Keep domain rules in testable server-side modules instead of route handlers or UI components. Separate persistence, external providers, and background work behind small interfaces without building a framework. Prefer ordinary HTML forms and URLs for durable navigation; use optimistic interaction only when failure can be reconciled clearly.

The product brief currently identifies Astro, Alpine, Durable Objects as capability context. Preserve any required native, browser-only, edge, storage, real-time, or background-processing capability through a narrow adapter appropriate to the selected framework. If the core workflow genuinely requires native or browser APIs, keep that runtime as the primary execution surface rather than simulating inaccessible capabilities or inventing an unnecessary web surface.

Integrate with WebSocket rooms. For every integration:
- List required environment variables in an .env.example without real secrets.
- Add a small adapter with timeouts, normalized errors, and a deterministic local fake or development path.
- Verify inbound signatures and deduplicate provider events where supported.
- Keep credentials server-side, encrypt long-lived provider tokens at rest, and redact secrets and sensitive payloads from logs.
- Define retry, backoff, and idempotency behavior for any side effect that can be repeated.

SECURITY AND PRIVACY
Validate coordinates and permissions server-side and cap board object counts.
Validate, normalize, and length-limit all untrusted input on the server. Escape rendered content by default, sanitize any intentionally accepted markup, rate-limit public or abuse-prone actions, and use private object storage plus short-lived authorized URLs for sensitive files. Collect the minimum personal data necessary for the named workflow. Document retention and deletion behavior. Add specific protections for the riskier surfaces in this app, such as uploads, redirects, outbound requests, email delivery, OAuth, webhooks, CSV import or export, and real-time connections.

ACCEPTANCE SCENARIOS
Automate these app-specific scenarios at the most appropriate level:
1. Given two participants move the same note nearly simultaneously, when both updates reach the room, every client converges on the server-accepted final position.
2. Given a three-dot voting round, when a participant attempts a fourth vote from another browser request, the server rejects it and totals remain unchanged.
3. Given a participant reconnects after missing several edits, when they provide their last revision, the board replays the gap without duplicating notes or votes.

Build just one piece

Not ready to replace all of Miro? Fine. These are the peculiar sub-problems hiding inside it — the parts that are actually interesting to build. Each one is a standalone prompt, scoped to an evening, no strings attached to the full build.

The board that replays what you missedMonotonic revisions, gap replay, and stale moves resolved predictably.
Build the synchronization core for a collaborative sticky-note board: one ordered log, everyone converges.

Every accepted mutation — add, move, edit, delete — passes server-side validation and gets stamped with the board's next revision number, a single monotonic sequence. Clients apply changes in revision order, so the current board is fully described by a state plus its revision. Reconnection is replay: the client presents its last revision and the server sends everything after it. Cap the replayable window — beyond it, send a fresh snapshot with its revision instead of a thousand-event catch-up.

Conflicts resolve by simple, predictable rules: for one note moved by two people, the later revision wins and every client converges on the server-accepted final position; a move arriving for a deleted note is rejected, and the mover's client reconciles on the rejection.

Client-generated operation ids make retries safe: resubmitting an unacknowledged mutation cannot apply twice.

Done when: two simultaneous moves of one note leave every client showing the identical position, a client reconnecting after fifty missed events replays to exact parity without duplicates, and no retry double-applies.
Three dots each, and the server is countingBudgets enforced server-side, hidden until reveal, refunded on deletion.
Build dot voting for workshop boards: three dots per person, enforced where it counts — the server.

The facilitator opens a round with a per-participant budget. Every vote request is validated server-side against the participant's remaining budget in the active round; the client's dot counter is decoration, not enforcement. A fourth vote attempted through a second browser tab, a replayed request, or a raw HTTP call gets rejected and changes nothing. Make the budget check and the vote insert atomic, so concurrent requests cannot both squeak under the cap.

Rules with teeth: exactly one active round per board; votes target existing notes only; a note deleted mid-round refunds its votes to their casters' budgets; and participants can retract their own dots while the round is open.

Totals stay hidden until the facilitator reveals — participants see only their own spent dots, so early votes cannot herd the room. Reveal freezes the round and shows totals per note and per frame, with ties displayed honestly rather than arbitrarily broken.

Done when: no request pattern exceeds the budget, deleting a voted note refunds correctly, and totals are invisible to participants until reveal.
Pan, zoom, and the math between screen and worldWorld versus screen coordinates, and zoom that pins the cursor.
Build pan and zoom for a sticky-note canvas — the coordinate math everything else sits on.

Keep two spaces strictly separate: world coordinates, where notes actually live and what the server stores, and screen coordinates, pixels in the viewport. The view is a transform — an offset and a scale — and every interaction converts through it. Zoom must center on the cursor: the world point under the pointer stays under the pointer as scale changes, which means adjusting the offset as a function of the zoom delta and cursor position. Get this wrong and zooming drifts toward a corner; users feel it instantly.

Clamp scale to sensible bounds, clamp panning so the bounded board cannot be flung entirely off-screen, and implement reset-view as a fit-all-content transform computed from the notes' bounding box. Hit-testing runs in world space, and dragging converts pointer deltas by the current scale so a note tracks the cursor precisely at any zoom.

Render a mini-map from the same transform math, inverted.

Done when: zooming at any cursor position keeps that world point stationary, notes track the pointer exactly at every zoom level, and reset-view frames all content with padding.
Anonymous to the room, accountable to the serverAuthorless in the UI, attributed in the data model, forever.
Build anonymous mode for a retrospective board: authorless to the room, fully attributed on the server.

When the facilitator enables it, notes display without author names or identifying colors. Internally, every note, edit, and vote keeps its participant id — anonymity is a presentation rule, not a data-model change. The server still needs identity for everything that matters: only a note's creator or the facilitator can edit or delete it, per-participant rate limits still apply, vote budgets still count, and the facilitator can still moderate someone pasting nonsense.

Then hunt the leaks: real-time events must not carry author ids to other participants' clients while anonymous mode is on — scrub payloads server-side, because hiding in the UI is not hiding. The JSON export omits authorship for anonymous contributions. And toggling anonymity off mid-session must not retroactively unmask notes written under the promise of anonymity: mark them permanently anonymous at creation time.

Done when: no client payload, export, or timing pattern reveals an anonymous author to participants, ownership checks and rate limits still bind, and notes written anonymously stay that way forever.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to build your own Miro?

A basic version — canvas, sticky notes, pan and zoom — takes about 12 hours. Roughly 1 week gets you a solid v1 with live cursors, dot voting, frames, exports. Matching everything Miro really does (freehand drawing, diagrams, hundreds of simultaneous editors) is closer to 3 months+, which is exactly why you should scope down instead.

How much does Miro cost if I keep subscribing?

Miro runs $10–$20 per person per month on public paid plans, which is $120–$240 per year for every person on your team. A focused self-built replacement costs your build time plus close-to-zero hosting.

What stack should I use to build a Miro alternative?

The build prompt on this page ships in four flavors: the AHA stack (Astro, HTMX, Alpine.js), Next.js, Laravel, and Ruby on Rails. The capability context for this product is Astro, Alpine, Durable Objects. Pick the stack you already know — the scope matters more than the framework.

What features does a minimal Miro replacement need?

A useful v1 needs: pan and zoom canvas with movable colored notes; named participants and visible cursors; timer, anonymous mode, and dot voting; frames for workshop sections and facilitator lock controls; export the finished board as PNG or structured JSON. Everything else is scope creep until you personally miss it.

What should I deliberately not build?

Do not support arbitrary drawing, diagrams, embeds, templates marketplace, or hundreds of simultaneous editors.

Does this build also replace Mural, FigJam and Lucidspark?

Yes. Miro, Mural, FigJam and Lucidspark all solve the same core job — let a group add, arrange, group, and vote on sticky notes in real time. The scoped v1 on this page covers what most people use any of them for, so one focused build replaces whichever you currently pay for.
