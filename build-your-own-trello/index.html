How to Vibe Code Your Own Trello Alternative

Cards move left. Cards move right. Work happens. Trello charges $10–$17.5 per person per month — that’s $120–$210 a year per person — for something you can replace with focused software of your own. Here is the honest scope, the honest timeline, and the exact prompt to hand your coding agent.

from 3 hoursClassic weekend win

Who this replacement is for

This build targets a small team managing one shared project board. The goal: make current work visible and easy to move from idea to done. If you need more than that, keep paying — the point of building it yourself is owning a tool shaped exactly like your workflow, not re-implementing a venture-funded roadmap.

Same build, different logo: this v1 also replaces Asana — they all solve the same core job.

How long it actually takes

One number would be a lie, so here are three. Each tier is a real, usable product — pick the one that matches how much of Trello you actually use.

EstimateWhat you get
3 hourscolumns, drag-and-drop cards
10 hourschecklists, labels, filters, activity timeline
1 month+real-time sync, power-ups, automation, mobile apps — the part you should probably skip

  • comments and a compact activity timeline
  • filters for member, label, overdue, and keyword
  • archive and restore for cards and columns

Data model

The guardrail

Deliberate non-goals

The complete build prompt

Copy this into your coding agent of choice. It is scoped for a useful v1 — journeys, screens, business rules, data model, security, tests, and acceptance scenarios included. Pick your stack:

You are building a production-ready software product named “Deck”, a deliberately focused alternative to Trello. Build a complete, usable vertical slice—not a landing page, static mockup, or disconnected collection of components.

WORKING AGREEMENT
Before writing implementation code, produce a short technical plan that names the routes or pages, server actions or endpoints, data tables, important state transitions, authorization boundaries, background jobs, and external adapters. Resolve contradictions in favor of the narrow audience and non-goals below. Prefer a small, legible architecture over speculative abstraction, but do not omit persistence, validation, error handling, or tests.

PRODUCT BRIEF
Primary user: a small team managing one shared project board.
Primary outcome: make current work visible and easy to move from idea to done.
Product principle: optimize the exact workflow below instead of copying the full breadth of Trello. A first-time user should understand what to do from the interface itself, without a tour or documentation.

END-TO-END USER JOURNEYS
Implement all of these flows through the real interface and persistent data layer:
1. The board owner creates a board, names three ordered columns, invites two teammates by email, and adds labeled cards with assignees, due dates, and checklists.
2. A teammate filters the board to their cards, opens one, completes checklist items, comments with an update, and drags the card from In progress to Done.
3. The owner archives an obsolete card and an empty column, reviews the activity trail, then restores the card to a chosen active column and position.

SCREENS AND INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE
Build these as coherent responsive views. Each screen must specify its primary action, secondary actions, visible status, validation feedback, empty state, loading or pending state, success confirmation, and recoverable failure state.
1. Boards dashboard: active and archived boards, member count, recent activity, create-board action, and pending invitation status.
2. Board view: horizontally ordered columns, card counts, draggable cards, add-card controls, member and label filters, keyword search, and overdue emphasis.
3. Card detail: title, markdown description, assignee, due date, labels, ordered checklist, comments, activity timeline, archive, and restore controls.
4. Board settings and archive: board name, members and roles, invitation management, column ordering, archived cards and columns, and permanent board-close action.

CORE CAPABILITIES
1. boards with ordered columns and drag-and-drop cards
2. card description, assignee, due date, labels, and checklist
3. comments and a compact activity timeline
4. filters for member, label, overdue, and keyword
5. archive and restore for cards and columns

DETAILED BEHAVIOR AND BUSINESS RULES
Treat these as server-enforced product requirements, not interface suggestions:
1. Authorize every board, column, card, checklist, comment, and archive mutation against active board membership on the server.
2. Store explicit integer positions for columns, cards, and checklist items; reorder affected siblings atomically and reject moves into archived columns.
3. Record card moves and meaningful field changes as immutable activity events containing actor, old value, new value, and timestamp.
4. Archiving is reversible and preserves comments and history; restoring requires a valid active destination and never silently recreates deleted membership.

DATA MODEL AND LIFECYCLE
Design a small relational schema centered on Board, Column, Card, ChecklistItem, Comment. Before implementing it, document:
1. Each table’s purpose, primary key, ownership or tenant boundary, timestamps, status fields, and important attributes.
2. Foreign keys, uniqueness constraints, check constraints, indexes needed by the named screens, and transaction boundaries for multi-record changes.
3. The allowed lifecycle or state transitions, who may trigger each transition, which transitions are terminal or reversible, and what audit history must remain immutable.
4. Archive, retention, and deletion behavior, including what happens to dependent records and external files.
5. Idempotency strategy for submissions, jobs, imports, notifications, webhooks, or retries where applicable.
Use migrations rather than ad-hoc schema creation. Store time instants consistently and retain named timezone context whenever local schedules or dates matter. Never rely on a counter, disabled button, or client-side check to preserve a business invariant.

USERS, AUTHENTICATION, AND PERMISSIONS
Implement only the roles required by the stated audience. Make the ownership and visibility model explicit before coding. Enforce authorization in every server-side query and mutation, including search, exports, attachments, live updates, and guessed URLs—not merely by hiding controls. Use secure session defaults, protect state-changing requests, and provide an understandable signed-out, expired-session, and forbidden state. Seed distinct users when multiple roles are required so permissions can be demonstrated and tested.

INTERACTION AND VISUAL DIRECTION
The product should feel fast, calm, focused, and credible rather than like a generic admin template. Use a clear visual hierarchy, restrained color, readable typography, generous hit targets, and consistent placement for primary actions. Start with server-rendered HTML and progressively enhance only the interactions that benefit from it. The core workflow must remain understandable if enhancement fails.

Start with server-rendered Astro pages and ordinary HTML forms. Use HTMX for form submissions, partial navigation, and server-driven updates, then Alpine.js only for small local browser state. The core workflow must remain understandable if either enhancement layer fails.

Design mobile layouts intentionally instead of simply stacking desktop panels. Support keyboard navigation, visible focus, semantic landmarks, explicit labels, useful page titles, reduced-motion preferences, and screen-reader announcements for asynchronous results. Never use color alone to communicate state. Destructive actions require clear scope and confirmation; safe repeated actions should be idempotent.

TECHNICAL DIRECTION
Build this version with the AHA stack: Astro for routing, layouts, and server-rendered pages; HTMX for interactions that benefit from HTML fragment responses; and Alpine.js for small, local interface state. Prefer Cloudflare D1 for relational persistence, R2 for object storage, Workers for server endpoints and scheduled work, Durable Objects only for coordinated real-time state, and Workflows or Queues for durable background jobs—but only when the product requirements call for them.

Keep domain rules in testable server-side modules instead of route handlers or UI components. Separate persistence, external providers, and background work behind small interfaces without building a framework. Prefer ordinary HTML forms and URLs for durable navigation; use optimistic interaction only when failure can be reconciled clearly.

The product brief currently identifies Astro, Alpine, D1 as capability context. Preserve any required native, browser-only, edge, storage, real-time, or background-processing capability through a narrow adapter appropriate to the selected framework. If the core workflow genuinely requires native or browser APIs, keep that runtime as the primary execution surface rather than simulating inaccessible capabilities or inventing an unnecessary web surface.

Integrate with email invite links. For every integration:
- List required environment variables in an .env.example without real secrets.
- Add a small adapter with timeouts, normalized errors, and a deterministic local fake or development path.
- Verify inbound signatures and deduplicate provider events where supported.
- Keep credentials server-side, encrypt long-lived provider tokens at rest, and redact secrets and sensitive payloads from logs.
- Define retry, backoff, and idempotency behavior for any side effect that can be repeated.

SECURITY AND PRIVACY
Enforce board membership on every mutation, not only in the interface.
Validate, normalize, and length-limit all untrusted input on the server. Escape rendered content by default, sanitize any intentionally accepted markup, rate-limit public or abuse-prone actions, and use private object storage plus short-lived authorized URLs for sensitive files. Collect the minimum personal data necessary for the named workflow. Document retention and deletion behavior. Add specific protections for the riskier surfaces in this app, such as uploads, redirects, outbound requests, email delivery, OAuth, webhooks, CSV import or export, and real-time connections.

ACCEPTANCE SCENARIOS
Automate these app-specific scenarios at the most appropriate level:
1. Given two members viewing the same board, when one moves a card, both views converge on the new column and position without duplicating the card.
2. Given a user whose board membership was removed, when they submit a previously loaded card edit, the request is denied and the card remains unchanged.
3. Given an archived card with completed checklist items and comments, when it is restored, all content returns intact and the restore appears in activity.

TESTING
Add focused unit tests for state transitions, authorization predicates, normalization, date or money calculations, and other risky domain rules. Add integration tests for persistence constraints and each external adapter’s success, timeout, retry, and rejection paths. Add at least one browser-level test for every end-to-end journey above, including one small-screen viewport. Tests must use isolated data and run through a documented single command.

OPERATIONS AND FAILURE RECOVERY
Add structured server logs with request, job, or event correlation IDs but no secrets or unnecessarily sensitive data. Make failures actionable in both the interface and logs. Background work must expose pending, succeeded, failed, and retrying states where relevant; do not silently swallow errors. Include safe database migration and rollback guidance, seed data, backup and restore notes, external-data cleanup behavior, and a basic health or diagnostic path appropriate to the stack.

DELIVERABLES
Ship the working application, migrations, representative seed data, tests, .env.example, and a concise README. The README must cover prerequisites, local setup, environment variables, migrations, seed and test commands, deployment, integration setup, backup and restore, security decisions, and known limitations. Seed data should exercise the happy path plus at least one empty, failed, overdue, expired, archived, or permission-restricted state relevant to the product.

DEFINITION OF DONE
The app is complete when a fresh developer can follow the README, create and migrate the database, run the app, sign in as each relevant role, complete every named journey using real persisted data, refresh without losing state, recover from common failures, and use the core interface on phone and desktop. All acceptance scenarios pass, permission boundaries are covered by tests, and no core screen is left as a placeholder.

NON-GOALS
Do not build workspace billing, Power-Ups, complex automation, or portfolio views.

Build just one piece

Not ready to replace all of Trello? Fine. These are the peculiar sub-problems hiding inside it — the parts that are actually interesting to build. Each one is a standalone prompt, scoped to an evening, no strings attached to the full build.

Card ordering that survives the ten-thousandth dragExplicit integers, atomic renumbering, and no fractional-position decay.
Build drag-and-drop card ordering for a kanban board that stays correct after ten thousand drags.

Store explicit integer positions per card within a column. A move — within a column or across columns — computes the target position and renumbers affected siblings in one transaction. At board scale, rewriting a column's positions on every move is fine, and beats clever fractional schemes that accumulate precision debt. Whatever you choose, the invariant is a gapless, duplicate-free ordering after any move.

The moves that break naive implementations: dropping at the very top and the very bottom, moving the only card out of a column, cross-column moves that must renumber two columns atomically, and a move racing a concurrent move of a neighboring card. Reject moves into archived columns with a visible error and a revert animation, never a silent snap.

The same routine powers checklist-item and column reordering: one reorder function, three uses.

Done when: any drag sequence leaves every column gapless and duplicate-free in the database, concurrent moves of different cards both land correctly, and archived columns refuse drops loudly.
Two people, one board, no duplicate cardsOptimistic drags, server arbitration, and clients that always converge.
Build multi-viewer consistency for a shared kanban board without building a realtime engine.

Two people have the same board open. One drags a card; the other should see it soon, and above all correctly. Use optimistic UI plus server reconciliation: the dragger's client applies the move instantly and sends a request carrying a client-generated operation id. The server is the single arbiter of order; other clients poll (or listen on a cheap channel) for changes since their last-seen board version and apply the difference.

The correctness traps: the dragger's own move echoing back must be recognized by operation id and not re-applied as a visual stutter; a rejected move — the card was archived meanwhile — must revert the optimistic state with an explanation; and two clients moving the same card converge on the server's answer, both ending identical. When versions gap too far, refetch-and-diff beats surgical patching.

Done when: a move appears on the second client within a poll cycle without duplicating the card, the mover never sees their own echo jitter, and deliberately conflicting moves end with both clients identical to the server.
The activity log that remembers old valuesImmutable events with old and new values, coalesced only at display time.
Build an activity timeline for a project board, recorded as immutable events with real old and new values.

Every meaningful mutation — card moved, assignee changed, due date set, checklist item completed, comment added, card archived — writes an event: actor, verb, old value, new value, timestamp. Write it in the same transaction as the change itself, or the log will eventually lie. Never update or delete events; corrections are new events.

Rendering is a compression problem: raw events are noisy, so coalesce at display time — five title edits within a minute by one actor collapse into one expandable entry. Renamed entities need care: the event stores values as they were at the time (column name, member name), so history reads correctly even after the column is renamed or the member removed from the board.

Support filtering by actor and event type. The card detail shows its own slice; the board view shows the merged stream.

Done when: every field change is reconstructable from events alone, display coalescing never hides that something happened, and events referencing renamed or deleted entities still render meaningfully.
Archive is a promise you can take backReversible by design, with restores that need a real destination.
Build archive and restore for kanban cards and columns: deletion with an undo, done properly.

Archiving is a reversible state, not a soft-delete flag bolted on later. An archived card keeps everything — comments, checklist state, labels, history — leaves the board view, stops matching filters, and lands in a browsable, searchable archive. Archiving a column archives its cards as one operation, remembering which cards were archived by the cascade versus already archived beforehand, because restoring the column must not resurrect cards someone deliberately archived last month.

Restore requires a valid destination: the original column if still active, otherwise an explicit choice. Position on restore appends to the column's end — honoring a stale position claim is how duplicate-position chaos starts. Both operations write activity events, and restoring never reinstates board memberships revoked while the card sat in the archive.

Permanent deletion exists only as a separate, confirmed action on already-archived items.

Done when: archive and restore round-trip a card with all content intact, a column cascade restores exactly the cards it archived, and no restore ever lands in an archived or missing column.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to build your own Trello?

A basic version — columns, drag-and-drop cards — takes about 3 hours. Roughly 10 hours gets you a solid v1 with checklists, labels, filters, activity timeline. Matching everything Trello really does (real-time sync, power-ups, automation, mobile apps) is closer to 1 month+, which is exactly why you should scope down instead.

How much does Trello cost if I keep subscribing?

Trello runs $10–$17.5 per person per month on public paid plans, which is $120–$210 per year for every person on your team. A focused self-built replacement costs your build time plus close-to-zero hosting.

What stack should I use to build a Trello alternative?

The build prompt on this page ships in four flavors: the AHA stack (Astro, HTMX, Alpine.js), Next.js, Laravel, and Ruby on Rails. The capability context for this product is Astro, Alpine, D1. Pick the stack you already know — the scope matters more than the framework.

What features does a minimal Trello replacement need?

A useful v1 needs: boards with ordered columns and drag-and-drop cards; card description, assignee, due date, labels, and checklist; comments and a compact activity timeline; filters for member, label, overdue, and keyword; archive and restore for cards and columns. Everything else is scope creep until you personally miss it.

What should I deliberately not build?

Do not build workspace billing, Power-Ups, complex automation, or portfolio views.

Does this build also replace Asana?

Yes. Trello, Asana all solve the same core job — make current work visible and easy to move from idea to done. The scoped v1 on this page covers what most people use any of them for, so one focused build replaces whichever you currently pay for.
