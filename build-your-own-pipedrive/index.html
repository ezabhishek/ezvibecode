How to Vibe Code Your Own Pipedrive Alternative

Move deals forward and make the next action impossible to miss. Pipedrive charges $24–$79 per person per month — that’s $288–$948 a year per person — for something you can replace with focused software of your own. Here is the honest scope, the honest timeline, and the exact prompt to hand your coding agent.

from 3 daysOne pipeline is enough

Who this replacement is for

This build targets a founder-led sales team managing one product and one sales process. The goal: see every live deal by stage and keep follow-ups from falling through. If you need more than that, keep paying — the point of building it yourself is owning a tool shaped exactly like your workflow, not re-implementing a venture-funded roadmap.

Same build, different logo: this v1 also replaces Close and Capsule — they all solve the same core job.

How long it actually takes

One number would be a lie, so here are three. Each tier is a real, usable product — pick the one that matches how much of Pipedrive you actually use.

EstimateWhat you get
3 daysdrag-and-drop pipeline, deal cards, next actions
2 weeks+activity timeline, stale-deal warnings, weighted pipeline reports
1 month+email sync, forecasting ai, mobile apps, automations — the part you should probably skip

What a minimal Pipedrive alternative needs

  • pipeline board with drag-and-drop stages
  • deal value, probability, owner, contact, and expected close date
  • next activity required for every active deal
  • notes, calls, meetings, and stage history timeline
  • stale-deal warnings plus weighted pipeline report

Data model

Person, Organization, Deal, Stage, Activity

Integrations

calendar links, email BCC capture

Capability context

Astro, Alpine, D1

The guardrail

Record stage changes atomically and require authorization for deal and export access.

Deliberate non-goals

Do not build lead marketplaces, email campaigns, phone dialers, forecasting AI, or multiple pipelines.

The complete build prompt

Copy this into your coding agent of choice. It is scoped for a useful v1 — journeys, screens, business rules, data model, security, tests, and acceptance scenarios included. Pick your stack:

You are building a production-ready software product named “Dealrail”, a deliberately focused alternative to Pipedrive. Build a complete, usable vertical slice—not a landing page, static mockup, or disconnected collection of components.

WORKING AGREEMENT
Before writing implementation code, produce a short technical plan that names the routes or pages, server actions or endpoints, data tables, important state transitions, authorization boundaries, background jobs, and external adapters. Resolve contradictions in favor of the narrow audience and non-goals below. Prefer a small, legible architecture over speculative abstraction, but do not omit persistence, validation, error handling, or tests.

PRODUCT BRIEF
Primary user: a founder-led sales team managing one product and one sales process.
Primary outcome: see every live deal by stage and keep follow-ups from falling through.
Product principle: optimize the exact workflow below instead of copying the full breadth of Pipedrive. A first-time user should understand what to do from the interface itself, without a tour or documentation.

END-TO-END USER JOURNEYS
Implement all of these flows through the real interface and persistent data layer:
1. A founder creates a deal with person, organization, value, probability, expected close date, stage, and a required next activity, then sees it on the pipeline board.
2. A salesperson works the activity agenda, completes a call, records the outcome, schedules the next meeting, and advances the deal with a complete timeline.
3. The founder reviews stale and overdue deals, updates or closes them as won or lost, and inspects the resulting weighted pipeline report.

SCREENS AND INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE
Build these as coherent responsive views. Each screen must specify its primary action, secondary actions, visible status, validation feedback, empty state, loading or pending state, success confirmation, and recoverable failure state.
1. Pipeline board: ordered stage columns, deal cards with value, probability, owner, next activity and close date, stale and overdue signals, filters, and drag controls.
2. Deal detail: person and organization, stage, value, probability, expected close, next activity, notes, calls, meetings, email summaries, and stage timeline.
3. Activity agenda: overdue, today, upcoming, and unscheduled groups; owner and activity-type filters; complete, reschedule, and open-deal actions.
4. Pipeline report: raw and weighted value by stage and owner, expected-close buckets, stale-deal list, won and lost totals, and CSV export.

CORE CAPABILITIES
1. pipeline board with drag-and-drop stages
2. deal value, probability, owner, contact, and expected close date
3. next activity required for every active deal
4. notes, calls, meetings, and stage history timeline
5. stale-deal warnings plus weighted pipeline report

DETAILED BEHAVIOR AND BUSINESS RULES
Treat these as server-enforced product requirements, not interface suggestions:
1. Every active deal must have one explicit next activity; completing or deleting it requires scheduling a replacement or closing the deal as won or lost.
2. Move stages atomically and append actor, previous stage, new stage, and time; apply the stage’s default probability only when no manual probability override exists.
3. Mark a deal stale when it has no future activity and no completed activity or stage change within the configured age, using one consistent business timezone.
4. Store amounts as integer minor units with an explicit currency, permission-filter all deal access, and calculate weighted value from the saved deal probability.

DATA MODEL AND LIFECYCLE
Design a small relational schema centered on Person, Organization, Deal, Stage, Activity. Before implementing it, document:
1. Each table’s purpose, primary key, ownership or tenant boundary, timestamps, status fields, and important attributes.
2. Foreign keys, uniqueness constraints, check constraints, indexes needed by the named screens, and transaction boundaries for multi-record changes.
3. The allowed lifecycle or state transitions, who may trigger each transition, which transitions are terminal or reversible, and what audit history must remain immutable.
4. Archive, retention, and deletion behavior, including what happens to dependent records and external files.
5. Idempotency strategy for submissions, jobs, imports, notifications, webhooks, or retries where applicable.
Use migrations rather than ad-hoc schema creation. Store time instants consistently and retain named timezone context whenever local schedules or dates matter. Never rely on a counter, disabled button, or client-side check to preserve a business invariant.

USERS, AUTHENTICATION, AND PERMISSIONS
Implement only the roles required by the stated audience. Make the ownership and visibility model explicit before coding. Enforce authorization in every server-side query and mutation, including search, exports, attachments, live updates, and guessed URLs—not merely by hiding controls. Use secure session defaults, protect state-changing requests, and provide an understandable signed-out, expired-session, and forbidden state. Seed distinct users when multiple roles are required so permissions can be demonstrated and tested.

INTERACTION AND VISUAL DIRECTION
The product should feel fast, calm, focused, and credible rather than like a generic admin template. Use a clear visual hierarchy, restrained color, readable typography, generous hit targets, and consistent placement for primary actions. Start with server-rendered HTML and progressively enhance only the interactions that benefit from it. The core workflow must remain understandable if enhancement fails.

Start with server-rendered Astro pages and ordinary HTML forms. Use HTMX for form submissions, partial navigation, and server-driven updates, then Alpine.js only for small local browser state. The core workflow must remain understandable if either enhancement layer fails.

Design mobile layouts intentionally instead of simply stacking desktop panels. Support keyboard navigation, visible focus, semantic landmarks, explicit labels, useful page titles, reduced-motion preferences, and screen-reader announcements for asynchronous results. Never use color alone to communicate state. Destructive actions require clear scope and confirmation; safe repeated actions should be idempotent.

TECHNICAL DIRECTION
Build this version with the AHA stack: Astro for routing, layouts, and server-rendered pages; HTMX for interactions that benefit from HTML fragment responses; and Alpine.js for small, local interface state. Prefer Cloudflare D1 for relational persistence, R2 for object storage, Workers for server endpoints and scheduled work, Durable Objects only for coordinated real-time state, and Workflows or Queues for durable background jobs—but only when the product requirements call for them.

Keep domain rules in testable server-side modules instead of route handlers or UI components. Separate persistence, external providers, and background work behind small interfaces without building a framework. Prefer ordinary HTML forms and URLs for durable navigation; use optimistic interaction only when failure can be reconciled clearly.

The product brief currently identifies Astro, Alpine, D1 as capability context. Preserve any required native, browser-only, edge, storage, real-time, or background-processing capability through a narrow adapter appropriate to the selected framework. If the core workflow genuinely requires native or browser APIs, keep that runtime as the primary execution surface rather than simulating inaccessible capabilities or inventing an unnecessary web surface.

Integrate with calendar links and email BCC capture. For every integration:
- List required environment variables in an .env.example without real secrets.
- Add a small adapter with timeouts, normalized errors, and a deterministic local fake or development path.
- Verify inbound signatures and deduplicate provider events where supported.
- Keep credentials server-side, encrypt long-lived provider tokens at rest, and redact secrets and sensitive payloads from logs.
- Define retry, backoff, and idempotency behavior for any side effect that can be repeated.

SECURITY AND PRIVACY
Record stage changes atomically and require authorization for deal and export access.
Validate, normalize, and length-limit all untrusted input on the server. Escape rendered content by default, sanitize any intentionally accepted markup, rate-limit public or abuse-prone actions, and use private object storage plus short-lived authorized URLs for sensitive files. Collect the minimum personal data necessary for the named workflow. Document retention and deletion behavior. Add specific protections for the riskier surfaces in this app, such as uploads, redirects, outbound requests, email delivery, OAuth, webhooks, CSV import or export, and real-time connections.

ACCEPTANCE SCENARIOS
Automate these app-specific scenarios at the most appropriate level:
1. Given an active deal with no next activity, saving is blocked with a focused prompt to schedule one or close the deal.
2. Given a completed call with a replacement meeting, the old activity remains in history and the meeting becomes the deal’s next action.
3. Given a deal moved to won, it leaves the active board, clears stale warnings, retains its complete timeline, and appears once in won reporting.

TESTING
Add focused unit tests for state transitions, authorization predicates, normalization, date or money calculations, and other risky domain rules. Add integration tests for persistence constraints and each external adapter’s success, timeout, retry, and rejection paths. Add at least one browser-level test for every end-to-end journey above, including one small-screen viewport. Tests must use isolated data and run through a documented single command.

OPERATIONS AND FAILURE RECOVERY
Add structured server logs with request, job, or event correlation IDs but no secrets or unnecessarily sensitive data. Make failures actionable in both the interface and logs. Background work must expose pending, succeeded, failed, and retrying states where relevant; do not silently swallow errors. Include safe database migration and rollback guidance, seed data, backup and restore notes, external-data cleanup behavior, and a basic health or diagnostic path appropriate to the stack.

DELIVERABLES
Ship the working application, migrations, representative seed data, tests, .env.example, and a concise README. The README must cover prerequisites, local setup, environment variables, migrations, seed and test commands, deployment, integration setup, backup and restore, security decisions, and known limitations. Seed data should exercise the happy path plus at least one empty, failed, overdue, expired, archived, or permission-restricted state relevant to the product.

DEFINITION OF DONE
The app is complete when a fresh developer can follow the README, create and migrate the database, run the app, sign in as each relevant role, complete every named journey using real persisted data, refresh without losing state, recover from common failures, and use the core interface on phone and desktop. All acceptance scenarios pass, permission boundaries are covered by tests, and no core screen is left as a placeholder.

NON-GOALS
Do not build lead marketplaces, email campaigns, phone dialers, forecasting AI, or multiple pipelines.

DETAILED BEHAVIOR AND BUSINESS RULES
Treat these as server-enforced product requirements, not interface suggestions:
1. Every active deal must have one explicit next activity; completing or deleting it requires scheduling a replacement or closing the deal as won or lost.
2. Move stages atomically and append actor, previous stage, new stage, and time; apply the stage’s default probability only when no manual probability override exists.
3. Mark a deal stale when it has no future activity and no completed activity or stage change within the configured age, using one consistent business timezone.
4. Store amounts as integer minor units with an explicit currency, permission-filter all deal access, and calculate weighted value from the saved deal probability.

3. The allowed lifecycle or state transitions, who may trigger each transition, which transitions are terminal or reversible, and what audit history must remain immutable.
4. Archive, retention, and deletion behavior, including what happens to dependent records and external files.

Build just one piece

Not ready to replace all of Pipedrive? Fine. These are the peculiar sub-problems hiding inside it — the parts that are actually interesting to build. Each one is a standalone prompt, scoped to an evening, no strings attached to the full build.

No deal without a next stepAn invariant, not a nag: active deals must always carry a future action.
Build the next-activity invariant for a sales pipeline: every active deal must have exactly one scheduled next activity, enforced structurally rather than by reminder.

Creating a deal requires scheduling its first activity in the same form — no activity, no save. Completing an activity triggers a focused prompt: schedule the replacement (defaulting to a sensible type and date) or close the deal as won or lost. There is no dismiss-and-decide-later path; that path is where follow-ups go to die, and this invariant is the product's entire reason to exist. Deleting a deal's only future activity gets the same treatment.

Enforce server-side, not just in the UI: any API write that would leave an active deal with zero future activities is rejected with a specific error. Bulk operations and imports go through the same check. Closed deals are exempt — won and lost deals need no next step.

On the board, render each card's next activity with urgency states: overdue in red, today highlighted, future quiet.

Done when: no sequence of UI or API operations produces an active deal without a future activity, and completing an activity always forks into replace-or-close.
The staleness detector with one clockA configured age, a single business timezone, and no false accusations.
Build stale-deal detection for a sales pipeline: surface deals quietly rotting before the quarter ends.

A deal is stale when two things are simultaneously true: it has no future scheduled activity, and nothing meaningful has happened within the configured staleness age — no completed activity, no stage change. Evaluate "meaningful" from the activity and stage history, not from any updated-at column, because a bulk field edit touching 50 deals must not launder their staleness.

Use one configured business timezone for all day arithmetic, so "14 days inactive" means the same thing for every deal and does not wobble around midnight depending on which server evaluated it. Make the threshold configurable per pipeline, defaulting to 14 days.

Compute staleness as a derived property evaluated on read or by a scheduled job — never a stored flag someone forgets to clear. Closing a deal as won or lost clears its warnings implicitly, since closed deals cannot be stale. Show stale deals as a badge on the board and a dedicated list sorted by days inactive.

Done when: a deal with a future meeting is never stale, a bulk edit does not reset staleness, and closing removes the warning without cleanup code.
Pipeline math in integer centsWeighted values from minor units, explicit currency, and probability precedence.
Build the weighted pipeline report for a sales tool — the number the founder checks weekly, so the math must be beyond suspicion.

Store every deal amount as integer minor units (cents) with an explicit currency code. No floats anywhere in money paths: floating-point drift across hundreds of summed deals makes reports wrong in the last digit. Sum in integers, format at the display edge.

Probability has a precedence rule: each stage defines a default probability, but a deal can carry a manual override. Weighted value uses the override when present, else the stage default — and critically, moving a deal to a new stage re-applies that stage's default only when no manual override exists. A rep who set 90 percent on their hot deal must not have it silently reset to the new stage's 40 by a drag.

Report weighted and raw value by stage and by owner, plus expected-close buckets (this month, next month, later, and overdue — past-dated closes flagged, not hidden). Refuse to sum mixed currencies into one number.

Done when: totals are exact under integer math, stage moves respect manual probability overrides, and mixed currencies render separately rather than as a fictional sum.
Drag a card, trust the resultOptimistic board moves that reconcile, revert, and never corrupt history.
Build the drag-and-drop pipeline board for a sales CRM: stage columns, deal cards, and moves that feel instant while staying correct.

On drop, move the card optimistically — the rep should never wait on a spinner — while the server processes the move atomically: update the stage, append a history entry (actor, previous stage, new stage, timestamp), and recalculate both affected column totals in one transaction. On failure (permission, validation, network), snap the card back to its origin with a visible error; on success, reconcile against the authoritative response.

Concurrency is the interesting part: two users viewing the same board, one moves a deal. Include a version on each move request so a move based on a stale position is rejected and refetched rather than applied blindly, and push updates so the second user's board reflects the move within seconds.

Get the physics right too: clear drop-zone highlighting, column auto-scroll when dragging near edges, keyboard-accessible moves as a fallback, and correct handling of a drop back onto the original column (a no-op, not a history entry).

Done when: a failed move reverts visibly, concurrent moves cannot silently overwrite each other, and every successful drag produces exactly one history entry.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to build your own Pipedrive?

A basic version — drag-and-drop pipeline, deal cards, next actions — takes about 3 days. Roughly 2 weeks+ gets you a solid v1 with activity timeline, stale-deal warnings, weighted pipeline reports. Matching everything Pipedrive really does (email sync, forecasting ai, mobile apps, automations) is closer to 1 month+, which is exactly why you should scope down instead.

How much does Pipedrive cost if I keep subscribing?

Pipedrive runs $24–$79 per person per month on public paid plans, which is $288–$948 per year for every person on your team. A focused self-built replacement costs your build time plus close-to-zero hosting.

What stack should I use to build a Pipedrive alternative?

The build prompt on this page ships in four flavors: the AHA stack (Astro, HTMX, Alpine.js), Next.js, Laravel, and Ruby on Rails. The capability context for this product is Astro, Alpine, D1. Pick the stack you already know — the scope matters more than the framework.

What features does a minimal Pipedrive replacement need?

A useful v1 needs: pipeline board with drag-and-drop stages; deal value, probability, owner, contact, and expected close date; next activity required for every active deal; notes, calls, meetings, and stage history timeline; stale-deal warnings plus weighted pipeline report. Everything else is scope creep until you personally miss it.

What should I deliberately not build?

Do not build lead marketplaces, email campaigns, phone dialers, forecasting AI, or multiple pipelines.

Does this build also replace Close and Capsule?

Yes. Pipedrive, Close and Capsule all solve the same core job — see every live deal by stage and keep follow-ups from falling through. The scoped v1 on this page covers what most people use any of them for, so one focused build replaces whichever you currently pay for.
