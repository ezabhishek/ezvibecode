How to Vibe Code Your Own Shopify Alternative

Sell a small catalog without becoming an ecommerce platform operator. Shopify charges $39–$399 per month — that’s $468–$4788 a year — for something you can replace with focused software of your own. Here is the honest scope, the honest timeline, and the exact prompt to hand your coding agent.

from 5 daysOne catalog, one checkout

Who this replacement is for

This build targets a maker selling fewer than 50 physical products in one country. The goal: publish a fast storefront, take payment through a hosted checkout, and fulfill simple orders. If you need more than that, keep paying — the point of building it yourself is owning a tool shaped exactly like your workflow, not re-implementing a venture-funded roadmap.

Same build, different logo: this v1 also replaces BigCommerce and Squarespace Commerce — they all solve the same core job.

How long it actually takes

One number would be a lie, so here are three. Each tier is a real, usable product — pick the one that matches how much of Shopify you actually use.

EstimateWhat you get
5 dayscatalog, cart, stripe checkout, order list
2 weeks+variants, inventory reservation, fulfillment states, order emails
3 months+tax engines, multi-currency, returns, fraud, app store — the part you should probably skip

What a minimal Shopify alternative needs

  • products with variants, price, inventory, images, and active state
  • searchable storefront, product page, cart, and stock-aware quantity controls
  • hosted checkout with shipping address and one flat-rate shipping rule
  • order dashboard with paid, packing, shipped, refunded, and cancelled states
  • customer confirmation and shipping emails

Data model

Product, Variant, InventoryAdjustment, Cart, Order, OrderLine

Integrations

Stripe Checkout, R2 object storage, transactional email

Capability context

Astro, HTMX, D1

The guardrail

Keep card data entirely in hosted checkout, verify payment webhooks, reserve inventory transactionally, and make fulfillment state changes auditable.

Deliberate non-goals

Do not calculate complex tax, run a marketplace, support subscriptions, multiple currencies, point of sale, returns automation, or carrier integrations.

The complete build prompt

Copy this into your coding agent of choice. It is scoped for a useful v1 — journeys, screens, business rules, data model, security, tests, and acceptance scenarios included. Pick your stack:

You are building a production-ready software product named “Tinycart”, a deliberately focused alternative to Shopify. Build a complete, usable vertical slice—not a landing page, static mockup, or disconnected collection of components.

WORKING AGREEMENT
Before writing implementation code, produce a short technical plan that names the routes or pages, server actions or endpoints, data tables, important state transitions, authorization boundaries, background jobs, and external adapters. Resolve contradictions in favor of the narrow audience and non-goals below. Prefer a small, legible architecture over speculative abstraction, but do not omit persistence, validation, error handling, or tests.

PRODUCT BRIEF
Primary user: a maker selling fewer than 50 physical products in one country.
Primary outcome: publish a fast storefront, take payment through a hosted checkout, and fulfill simple orders.
Product principle: optimize the exact workflow below instead of copying the full breadth of Shopify. A first-time user should understand what to do from the interface itself, without a tour or documentation.

END-TO-END USER JOURNEYS
Implement all of these flows through the real interface and persistent data layer:
1. The merchant creates a product with two variants, uploads images, sets inventory and a flat shipping rate, previews the storefront, and publishes it.
2. A shopper searches the catalog, adds an in-stock variant to the cart, completes hosted checkout, and receives an order confirmation exactly once.
3. The merchant opens the paid order, marks it packing and shipped with tracking text, sends the update, then issues a full refund through the payment provider.

SCREENS AND INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE
Build these as coherent responsive views. Each screen must specify its primary action, secondary actions, visible status, validation feedback, empty state, loading or pending state, success confirmation, and recoverable failure state.
1. Product admin: product list, active state, variant matrix, prices, inventory, images, validation, preview, and publish actions.
2. Storefront and product: search, stock status, variant selection, quantity, image gallery, cart feedback, empty states, and accessible checkout path.
3. Cart and hosted checkout handoff: lines, quantities, shipping estimate, total, stock recheck, pending-payment state, return success, and cancelled checkout.
4. Orders: paid and fulfillment filters, customer and address, immutable lines and totals, timeline, packing, shipping, cancellation, refund, and notification state.

CORE CAPABILITIES
1. products with variants, price, inventory, images, and active state
2. searchable storefront, product page, cart, and stock-aware quantity controls
3. hosted checkout with shipping address and one flat-rate shipping rule
4. order dashboard with paid, packing, shipped, refunded, and cancelled states
5. customer confirmation and shipping emails

DETAILED BEHAVIOR AND BUSINESS RULES
Treat these as server-enforced product requirements, not interface suggestions:
1. Store money as integer minor units in one configured currency and snapshot product, variant, price, and shipping data onto order lines at checkout creation.
2. Reserve inventory transactionally for a bounded checkout window, release expired reservations idempotently, and never allow paid stock to fall below zero.
3. Treat verified payment webhooks as authoritative, deduplicate every provider event, and send confirmations only after the paid transition commits.
4. Enforce explicit paid, packing, shipped, cancelled, and refunded transitions with immutable history; execute refunds through the provider before recording success.

DATA MODEL AND LIFECYCLE
Design a small relational schema centered on Product, Variant, InventoryAdjustment, Cart, Order, OrderLine. Before implementing it, document:
1. Each table’s purpose, primary key, ownership or tenant boundary, timestamps, status fields, and important attributes.
2. Foreign keys, uniqueness constraints, check constraints, indexes needed by the named screens, and transaction boundaries for multi-record changes.
3. The allowed lifecycle or state transitions, who may trigger each transition, which transitions are terminal or reversible, and what audit history must remain immutable.
4. Archive, retention, and deletion behavior, including what happens to dependent records and external files.
5. Idempotency strategy for submissions, jobs, imports, notifications, webhooks, or retries where applicable.
Use migrations rather than ad-hoc schema creation. Store time instants consistently and retain named timezone context whenever local schedules or dates matter. Never rely on a counter, disabled button, or client-side check to preserve a business invariant.

USERS, AUTHENTICATION, AND PERMISSIONS
Implement only the roles required by the stated audience. Make the ownership and visibility model explicit before coding. Enforce authorization in every server-side query and mutation, including search, exports, attachments, live updates, and guessed URLs—not merely by hiding controls. Use secure session defaults, protect state-changing requests, and provide an understandable signed-out, expired-session, and forbidden state. Seed distinct users when multiple roles are required so permissions can be demonstrated and tested.

INTERACTION AND VISUAL DIRECTION
The product should feel fast, calm, focused, and credible rather than like a generic admin template. Use a clear visual hierarchy, restrained color, readable typography, generous hit targets, and consistent placement for primary actions. Start with server-rendered HTML and progressively enhance only the interactions that benefit from it. The core workflow must remain understandable if enhancement fails.

Start with server-rendered Astro pages and ordinary HTML forms. Use HTMX for form submissions, partial navigation, and server-driven updates, then Alpine.js only for small local browser state. The core workflow must remain understandable if either enhancement layer fails.

Design mobile layouts intentionally instead of simply stacking desktop panels. Support keyboard navigation, visible focus, semantic landmarks, explicit labels, useful page titles, reduced-motion preferences, and screen-reader announcements for asynchronous results. Never use color alone to communicate state. Destructive actions require clear scope and confirmation; safe repeated actions should be idempotent.

TECHNICAL DIRECTION
Build this version with the AHA stack: Astro for routing, layouts, and server-rendered pages; HTMX for interactions that benefit from HTML fragment responses; and Alpine.js for small, local interface state. Prefer Cloudflare D1 for relational persistence, R2 for object storage, Workers for server endpoints and scheduled work, Durable Objects only for coordinated real-time state, and Workflows or Queues for durable background jobs—but only when the product requirements call for them.

Keep domain rules in testable server-side modules instead of route handlers or UI components. Separate persistence, external providers, and background work behind small interfaces without building a framework. Prefer ordinary HTML forms and URLs for durable navigation; use optimistic interaction only when failure can be reconciled clearly.

The product brief currently identifies Astro, HTMX, D1 as capability context. Preserve any required native, browser-only, edge, storage, real-time, or background-processing capability through a narrow adapter appropriate to the selected framework. If the core workflow genuinely requires native or browser APIs, keep that runtime as the primary execution surface rather than simulating inaccessible capabilities or inventing an unnecessary web surface.

Integrate with Stripe Checkout and R2 object storage and transactional email. For every integration:
- List required environment variables in an .env.example without real secrets.
- Add a small adapter with timeouts, normalized errors, and a deterministic local fake or development path.
- Verify inbound signatures and deduplicate provider events where supported.
- Keep credentials server-side, encrypt long-lived provider tokens at rest, and redact secrets and sensitive payloads from logs.
- Define retry, backoff, and idempotency behavior for any side effect that can be repeated.

SECURITY AND PRIVACY
Keep card data entirely in hosted checkout, verify payment webhooks, reserve inventory transactionally, and make fulfillment state changes auditable.
Validate, normalize, and length-limit all untrusted input on the server. Escape rendered content by default, sanitize any intentionally accepted markup, rate-limit public or abuse-prone actions, and use private object storage plus short-lived authorized URLs for sensitive files. Collect the minimum personal data necessary for the named workflow. Document retention and deletion behavior. Add specific protections for the riskier surfaces in this app, such as uploads, redirects, outbound requests, email delivery, OAuth, webhooks, CSV import or export, and real-time connections.

ACCEPTANCE SCENARIOS
Automate these app-specific scenarios at the most appropriate level:
1. Given one unit remains and two shoppers attempt checkout, at most one reservation succeeds and the other receives a recoverable out-of-stock state.
2. Given the same paid webhook is delivered three times, one order becomes paid and one confirmation is sent.
3. Given a refund provider timeout, the order is not labeled refunded until reconciliation confirms the external result.

TESTING
Add focused unit tests for state transitions, authorization predicates, normalization, date or money calculations, and other risky domain rules. Add integration tests for persistence constraints and each external adapter’s success, timeout, retry, and rejection paths. Add at least one browser-level test for every end-to-end journey above, including one small-screen viewport. Tests must use isolated data and run through a documented single command.

OPERATIONS AND FAILURE RECOVERY
Add structured server logs with request, job, or event correlation IDs but no secrets or unnecessarily sensitive data. Make failures actionable in both the interface and logs. Background work must expose pending, succeeded, failed, and retrying states where relevant; do not silently swallow errors. Include safe database migration and rollback guidance, seed data, backup and restore notes, external-data cleanup behavior, and a basic health or diagnostic path appropriate to the stack.

DELIVERABLES
Ship the working application, migrations, representative seed data, tests, .env.example, and a concise README. The README must cover prerequisites, local setup, environment variables, migrations, seed and test commands, deployment, integration setup, backup and restore, security decisions, and known limitations. Seed data should exercise the happy path plus at least one empty, failed, overdue, expired, archived, or permission-restricted state relevant to the product.

DEFINITION OF DONE
The app is complete when a fresh developer can follow the README, create and migrate the database, run the app, sign in as each relevant role, complete every named journey using real persisted data, refresh without losing state, recover from common failures, and use the core interface on phone and desktop. All acceptance scenarios pass, permission boundaries are covered by tests, and no core screen is left as a placeholder.

NON-GOALS
Do not calculate complex tax, run a marketplace, support subscriptions, multiple currencies, point of sale, returns automation, or carrier integrations.

Before writing implementation code, produce a short technical plan that names the routes or pages, server actions or endpoints, data tables, important state transitions, authorization boundaries, background jobs, and external adapters. Resolve contradictions in favor of the narrow audience and non-goals below. Prefer a small, legible architecture over speculative abstraction, but do not omit persistence, validation, error handling, or tests.

PRODUCT BRIEF
Primary user: a maker selling fewer than 50 physical products in one country.
Primary outcome: publish a fast storefront, take payment through a hosted checkout, and fulfill simple orders.
Product principle: optimize the exact workflow below instead of copying the full breadth of Shopify. A first-time user should understand what to do from the interface itself, without a tour or documentation.

END-TO-END USER JOURNEYS
Implement all of these flows through the real interface and persistent data layer:
1. The merchant creates a product with two variants, uploads images, sets inventory and a flat shipping rate, previews the storefront, and publishes it.
2. A shopper searches the catalog, adds an in-stock variant to the cart, completes hosted checkout, and receives an order confirmation exactly once.
3. The merchant opens the paid order, marks it packing and shipped with tracking text, sends the update, then issues a full refund through the payment provider.

SCREENS AND INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE
Build these as coherent responsive views. Each screen must specify its primary action, secondary actions, visible status, validation feedback, empty state, loading or pending state, success confirmation, and recoverable failure state.
1. Product admin: product list, active state, variant matrix, prices, inventory, images, validation, preview, and publish actions.
2. Storefront and product: search, stock status, variant selection, quantity, image gallery, cart feedback, empty states, and accessible checkout path.
3. Cart and hosted checkout handoff: lines, quantities, shipping estimate, total, stock recheck, pending-payment state, return success, and cancelled checkout.
4. Orders: paid and fulfillment filters, customer and address, immutable lines and totals, timeline, packing, shipping, cancellation, refund, and notification state.

CORE CAPABILITIES

4. order dashboard with paid, packing, shipped, refunded, and cancelled states

DETAILED BEHAVIOR AND BUSINESS RULES
Treat these as server-enforced product requirements, not interface suggestions:
1. Store money as integer minor units in one configured currency and snapshot product, variant, price, and shipping data onto order lines at checkout creation.
2. Reserve inventory transactionally for a bounded checkout window, release expired reservations idempotently, and never allow paid stock to fall below zero.
3. Treat verified payment webhooks as authoritative, deduplicate every provider event, and send confirmations only after the paid transition commits.
4. Enforce explicit paid, packing, shipped, cancelled, and refunded transitions with immutable history; execute refunds through the provider before recording success.

DATA MODEL AND LIFECYCLE
Design a small relational schema centered on Product, Variant, InventoryAdjustment, Cart, Order, OrderLine. Before implementing it, document:
1. Each table’s purpose, primary key, ownership or tenant boundary, timestamps, status fields, and important attributes.
2. Foreign keys, uniqueness constraints, check constraints, indexes needed by the named screens, and transaction boundaries for multi-record changes.
3. The allowed lifecycle or state transitions, who may trigger each transition, which transitions are terminal or reversible, and what audit history must remain immutable.
4. Archive, retention, and deletion behavior, including what happens to dependent records and external files.
5. Idempotency strategy for submissions, jobs, imports, notifications, webhooks, or retries where applicable.
Use migrations rather than ad-hoc schema creation. Store time instants consistently and retain named timezone context whenever local schedules or dates matter. Never rely on a counter, disabled button, or client-side check to preserve a business invariant.

USERS, AUTHENTICATION, AND PERMISSIONS
Implement only the roles required by the stated audience. Make the ownership and visibility model explicit before coding. Enforce authorization in every server-side query and mutation, including search, exports, attachments, live updates, and guessed URLs—not merely by hiding controls. Use secure session defaults, protect state-changing requests, and provide an understandable signed-out, expired-session, and forbidden state. Seed distinct users when multiple roles are required so permissions can be demonstrated and tested.

INTERACTION AND VISUAL DIRECTION

Start with server-rendered Rails views and ordinary forms. Use Turbo for navigation, submissions, and server-driven updates, then Stimulus only for small browser-side behavior. Do not turn the product into a client-side SPA, and keep the core workflow usable when enhancement fails.

Build this version with Ruby on Rails and Hotwire: Turbo for navigation, form submissions, and server-driven updates, and Stimulus for small browser-side behavior. Build just one piece

Not ready to replace all of Shopify? Fine. These are the peculiar sub-problems hiding inside it — the parts that are actually interesting to build. Each one is a standalone prompt, scoped to an evening, no strings attached to the full build.

The last unit and the two shoppers who want itTransactional inventory reservation with a checkout window that expires idempotently.
Build inventory reservation for a small shop's checkout.

When a shopper starts checkout, reserve their cart's quantities transactionally: decrement available stock and create a reservation with an expiry, say 15 minutes, in one atomic operation that fails if stock is insufficient. Two shoppers racing for the last unit must resolve to exactly one reservation and one clean out-of-stock message with a recoverable cart, never a negative stock count and never two paid orders for one item.

Expiry must be idempotent: a sweeper releases expired reservations back to stock, and running it twice, or racing it against the shopper completing payment at the last second, must not double-release or release a reservation that just converted to an order. Use state transitions, active, converted, released, guarded by compare-and-swap semantics.

Done when: a concurrency test with ten shoppers and three units produces exactly three reservations, expired reservations return stock exactly once under repeated sweeps, and payment completing at expiry time either converts or fails cleanly, never both.
The webhook that arrives three timesVerify, deduplicate, transition once, email once — payment events are at-least-once delivery.
Build the payment webhook handler for a shop using a hosted checkout provider.

Providers deliver events at-least-once, so the same payment-succeeded event will arrive twice, sometimes three times, sometimes out of order with other events. First verify the signature against the provider's signing secret, rejecting anything unverifiable before parsing. Then deduplicate: record every processed event id in a table with a unique constraint, and treat a duplicate insert as already handled, return success so the provider stops retrying.

The paid transition and its side effects must be exactly-once: mark the order paid inside a transaction that also records the event id, and enqueue the confirmation email only after that transaction commits, keyed by order id so even a duplicate enqueue sends one email. Handle events for unknown orders, log and acknowledge, and out-of-order events, a refund event arriving before you processed the payment, by checking current state before transitioning.

Done when: replaying the same paid webhook five times yields one paid order and one email, an unverifiable signature is rejected with no state change, and out-of-order events never produce an illegal state transition.
The order that remembers what the price wasSnapshot everything onto order lines — future edits must never rewrite a receipt.
Build order-line snapshotting for a small shop.

An order is a historical record, not a view over live data. At checkout creation, copy everything the order needs onto its lines: product name, variant description, unit price in integer minor units, quantity, image reference, plus the shipping rate and its label. After that, the merchant renaming the product, changing its price, deleting the variant, or retiring the shipping rule must change nothing about the order, its emails, or its exports.

The subtle cases: the confirmation email rendered from the order must use snapshotted values, not a fresh product lookup; a reorder feature maps snapshots back to live products and honestly reports what no longer exists or costs more now; totals stored on the order, subtotal, shipping, grand total, are computed once at creation and never recomputed from lines at read time.

Done when: deleting a product leaves its past orders rendering perfectly with names, prices, and totals intact, a price change after checkout creation doesn't alter the order total, and stored totals always equal the sum of snapshotted lines.
Refunded means the money actually movedNever mark an order refunded on a timeout — reconcile with the provider first.
Build the refund flow for a shop using an external payment provider.

Refunds are two-system operations and the failure mode is disagreement. When the merchant clicks refund, record a refund attempt in a pending state, then call the provider. On confirmed success, transition to refunded and log the provider's refund id. On explicit failure, mark failed with the reason. On timeout or an ambiguous network error, the dangerous case, stay pending: the provider may or may not have processed it, so neither retry blindly, risking a double refund, nor mark refunded, risking lying to accounting.

Resolve pending attempts through reconciliation: query the provider's API for the payment's refund list, match by an idempotency key you sent with the original request, and settle the local state from what the provider reports. Use that same idempotency key on any retry.

Done when: a timeout leaves the order visibly pending-refund rather than refunded, reconciliation converges local state to the provider's truth in both outcomes, and no sequence of retries can issue two refunds for one order.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to build your own Shopify?

A basic version — catalog, cart, stripe checkout, order list — takes about 5 days. Roughly 2 weeks+ gets you a solid v1 with variants, inventory reservation, fulfillment states, order emails. Matching everything Shopify really does (tax engines, multi-currency, returns, fraud, app store) is closer to 3 months+, which is exactly why you should scope down instead.

How much does Shopify cost if I keep subscribing?

Shopify runs $39–$399 per month on public paid plans, which is $468–$4788 per year. A focused self-built replacement costs your build time plus close-to-zero hosting.

What stack should I use to build a Shopify alternative?

The build prompt on this page ships in four flavors: the AHA stack (Astro, HTMX, Alpine.js), Next.js, Laravel, and Ruby on Rails. The capability context for this product is Astro, HTMX, D1. Pick the stack you already know — the scope matters more than the framework.

What features does a minimal Shopify replacement need?

A useful v1 needs: products with variants, price, inventory, images, and active state; searchable storefront, product page, cart, and stock-aware quantity controls; hosted checkout with shipping address and one flat-rate shipping rule; order dashboard with paid, packing, shipped, refunded, and cancelled states; customer confirmation and shipping emails. Everything else is scope creep until you personally miss it.

What should I deliberately not build?

Do not calculate complex tax, run a marketplace, support subscriptions, multiple currencies, point of sale, returns automation, or carrier integrations.

Does this build also replace BigCommerce and Squarespace Commerce?

Yes. Shopify, BigCommerce and Squarespace Commerce all solve the same core job — publish a fast storefront, take payment through a hosted checkout, and fulfill simple orders. The scoped v1 on this page covers what most people use any of them for, so one focused build replaces whichever you currently pay for.
