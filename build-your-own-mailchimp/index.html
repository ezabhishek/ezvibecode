How to Vibe Code Your Own Mailchimp Alternative

One list, a few tags, and newsletters that arrive. Mailchimp charges $20–$350 per month — that’s $240–$4200 a year — for something you can replace with focused software of your own. Here is the honest scope, the honest timeline, and the exact prompt to hand your coding agent.

from 12 hoursIf your list is simple

Who this replacement is for

This build targets a small company sending a weekly newsletter to one audience. The goal: manage consented subscribers and send reliable, measurable broadcasts. If you need more than that, keep paying — the point of building it yourself is owning a tool shaped exactly like your workflow, not re-implementing a venture-funded roadmap.

Same build, different logo: this v1 also replaces MailerLite, Brevo, ActiveCampaign and Constant Contact — they all solve the same core job.

How long it actually takes

One number would be a lie, so here are three. Each tier is a real, usable product — pick the one that matches how much of Mailchimp you actually use.

EstimateWhat you get
12 hourssubscriber list, send a broadcast
4 daysdouble opt-in, tags, scheduling, open tracking
3 months+visual journeys, template builder, full crm — the part you should probably skip

What a minimal Mailchimp alternative needs

  • subscribe form with double opt-in and unsubscribe
  • subscriber table with tags, status, source, and CSV import
  • plain, markdown, or simple HTML campaign editor
  • test send, schedule, send progress, and failure retry
  • delivery, open, and click summary with privacy-aware tracking

Data model

Subscriber, Tag, Campaign, Delivery, LinkClick

Integrations

transactional email provider, Cloudflare Cron Triggers

Capability context

Astro, D1, Workers

The guardrail

Honor unsubscribe immediately, keep consent evidence, and authenticate sending domains.

Deliberate non-goals

Do not build visual automation journeys, a template marketplace, multivariate tests, or a full CRM.

The complete build prompt

Copy this into your coding agent of choice. It is scoped for a useful v1 — journeys, screens, business rules, data model, security, tests, and acceptance scenarios included. Pick your stack:

You are building a production-ready software product named “Postbird”, a deliberately focused alternative to Mailchimp. Build a complete, usable vertical slice—not a landing page, static mockup, or disconnected collection of components.

WORKING AGREEMENT
Before writing implementation code, produce a short technical plan that names the routes or pages, server actions or endpoints, data tables, important state transitions, authorization boundaries, background jobs, and external adapters. Resolve contradictions in favor of the narrow audience and non-goals below. Prefer a small, legible architecture over speculative abstraction, but do not omit persistence, validation, error handling, or tests.

PRODUCT BRIEF
Primary user: a small company sending a weekly newsletter to one audience.
Primary outcome: manage consented subscribers and send reliable, measurable broadcasts.
Product principle: optimize the exact workflow below instead of copying the full breadth of Mailchimp. A first-time user should understand what to do from the interface itself, without a tour or documentation.

END-TO-END USER JOURNEYS
Implement all of these flows through the real interface and persistent data layer:
1. A visitor submits the hosted signup form, receives a double-opt-in email, confirms through a single-use link, and can later unsubscribe immediately.
2. The owner imports a CSV, maps email and tags, reviews duplicates and invalid rows, confirms the valid subset, and filters the resulting audience by tag and status.
3. The owner writes a markdown newsletter, previews rendered email, sends a test, chooses recipients, schedules it, monitors deliveries and retries, then reviews privacy-aware results.

SCREENS AND INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE
Build these as coherent responsive views. Each screen must specify its primary action, secondary actions, visible status, validation feedback, empty state, loading or pending state, success confirmation, and recoverable failure state.
1. Audience: searchable subscriber table with status, tags, source, consent time, import action, row detail, suppression reason, and CSV export.
2. Campaign editor: subject, preview text, markdown or simple HTML body, reusable header and footer, rendered preview, link validation, autosave, and test-send action.
3. Send review and progress: immutable content preview, recipient criteria and count, sending-domain status, schedule in owner timezone, confirmation, delivery progress, and retryable failures.
4. Campaign report and forms: delivered, bounced, opened, and clicked summaries, top links, failure reasons, hosted subscribe-form settings, and unsubscribe confirmation preview.

CORE CAPABILITIES
1. subscribe form with double opt-in and unsubscribe
2. subscriber table with tags, status, source, and CSV import
3. plain, markdown, or simple HTML campaign editor
4. test send, schedule, send progress, and failure retry
5. delivery, open, and click summary with privacy-aware tracking

DETAILED BEHAVIOR AND BUSINESS RULES
Treat these as server-enforced product requirements, not interface suggestions:
1. Normalize email addresses for uniqueness, preserve consent source and timestamps, use single-use expiring confirmation tokens, and never send campaigns to unconfirmed subscribers.
2. CSV import must dry-run mappings and row errors, escape spreadsheet formulas on export, merge only explicitly selected fields, and never silently resubscribe suppressed addresses.
3. Freeze campaign subject, body, links, recipient query, and recipient snapshot when scheduling begins; create one idempotent delivery record per campaign and subscriber.
4. Apply unsubscribe and hard-bounce suppression before every send attempt, process provider events idempotently, and use privacy-aware open and click tracking with clear limitations.

DATA MODEL AND LIFECYCLE
Design a small relational schema centered on Subscriber, Tag, Campaign, Delivery, LinkClick. Before implementing it, document:
1. Each table’s purpose, primary key, ownership or tenant boundary, timestamps, status fields, and important attributes.
2. Foreign keys, uniqueness constraints, check constraints, indexes needed by the named screens, and transaction boundaries for multi-record changes.
3. The allowed lifecycle or state transitions, who may trigger each transition, which transitions are terminal or reversible, and what audit history must remain immutable.
4. Archive, retention, and deletion behavior, including what happens to dependent records and external files.
5. Idempotency strategy for submissions, jobs, imports, notifications, webhooks, or retries where applicable.
Use migrations rather than ad-hoc schema creation. Store time instants consistently and retain named timezone context whenever local schedules or dates matter. Never rely on a counter, disabled button, or client-side check to preserve a business invariant.

USERS, AUTHENTICATION, AND PERMISSIONS
Implement only the roles required by the stated audience. Make the ownership and visibility model explicit before coding. Enforce authorization in every server-side query and mutation, including search, exports, attachments, live updates, and guessed URLs—not merely by hiding controls. Use secure session defaults, protect state-changing requests, and provide an understandable signed-out, expired-session, and forbidden state. Seed distinct users when multiple roles are required so permissions can be demonstrated and tested.

INTERACTION AND VISUAL DIRECTION
The product should feel fast, calm, focused, and credible rather than like a generic admin template. Use a clear visual hierarchy, restrained color, readable typography, generous hit targets, and consistent placement for primary actions. Start with server-rendered HTML and progressively enhance only the interactions that benefit from it. The core workflow must remain understandable if enhancement fails.

Start with server-rendered Astro pages and ordinary HTML forms. Use HTMX for form submissions, partial navigation, and server-driven updates, then Alpine.js only for small local browser state. The core workflow must remain understandable if either enhancement layer fails.

Design mobile layouts intentionally instead of simply stacking desktop panels. Support keyboard navigation, visible focus, semantic landmarks, explicit labels, useful page titles, reduced-motion preferences, and screen-reader announcements for asynchronous results. Never use color alone to communicate state. Destructive actions require clear scope and confirmation; safe repeated actions should be idempotent.

TECHNICAL DIRECTION
Build this version with the AHA stack: Astro for routing, layouts, and server-rendered pages; HTMX for interactions that benefit from HTML fragment responses; and Alpine.js for small, local interface state. Prefer Cloudflare D1 for relational persistence, R2 for object storage, Workers for server endpoints and scheduled work, Durable Objects only for coordinated real-time state, and Workflows or Queues for durable background jobs—but only when the product requirements call for them.

Keep domain rules in testable server-side modules instead of route handlers or UI components. Separate persistence, external providers, and background work behind small interfaces without building a framework. Prefer ordinary HTML forms and URLs for durable navigation; use optimistic interaction only when failure can be reconciled clearly.

The product brief currently identifies Astro, D1, Workers as capability context. Preserve any required native, browser-only, edge, storage, real-time, or background-processing capability through a narrow adapter appropriate to the selected framework. If the core workflow genuinely requires native or browser APIs, keep that runtime as the primary execution surface rather than simulating inaccessible capabilities or inventing an unnecessary web surface.

Integrate with transactional email provider and Cloudflare Cron Triggers. For every integration:
- List required environment variables in an .env.example without real secrets.
- Add a small adapter with timeouts, normalized errors, and a deterministic local fake or development path.
- Verify inbound signatures and deduplicate provider events where supported.
- Keep credentials server-side, encrypt long-lived provider tokens at rest, and redact secrets and sensitive payloads from logs.
- Define retry, backoff, and idempotency behavior for any side effect that can be repeated.

SECURITY AND PRIVACY
Honor unsubscribe immediately, keep consent evidence, and authenticate sending domains.
Validate, normalize, and length-limit all untrusted input on the server. Escape rendered content by default, sanitize any intentionally accepted markup, rate-limit public or abuse-prone actions, and use private object storage plus short-lived authorized URLs for sensitive files. Collect the minimum personal data necessary for the named workflow. Document retention and deletion behavior. Add specific protections for the riskier surfaces in this app, such as uploads, redirects, outbound requests, email delivery, OAuth, webhooks, CSV import or export, and real-time connections.

ACCEPTANCE SCENARIOS
Automate these app-specific scenarios at the most appropriate level:
1. Given an unconfirmed signup, when the owner sends a campaign, no delivery is created until the subscriber completes the single-use confirmation link.
2. Given a subscriber unsubscribes while a scheduled campaign is being processed, when their delivery reaches the worker, suppression wins and no email is sent.
3. Given a provider timeout after accepting a message, when the worker retries with the same delivery identity, the subscriber receives at most one campaign email.

TESTING
Add focused unit tests for state transitions, authorization predicates, normalization, date or money calculations, and other risky domain rules. Add integration tests for persistence constraints and each external adapter’s success, timeout, retry, and rejection paths. Add at least one browser-level test for every end-to-end journey above, including one small-screen viewport. Tests must use isolated data and run through a documented single command.

OPERATIONS AND FAILURE RECOVERY
Add structured server logs with request, job, or event correlation IDs but no secrets or unnecessarily sensitive data. Make failures actionable in both the interface and logs. Background work must expose pending, succeeded, failed, and retrying states where relevant; do not silently swallow errors. Include safe database migration and rollback guidance, seed data, backup and restore notes, external-data cleanup behavior, and a basic health or diagnostic path appropriate to the stack.

DELIVERABLES
Ship the working application, migrations, representative seed data, tests, .env.example, and a concise README. The README must cover prerequisites, local setup, environment variables, migrations, seed and test commands, deployment, integration setup, backup and restore, security decisions, and known limitations. Seed data should exercise the happy path plus at least one empty, failed, overdue, expired, archived, or permission-restricted state relevant to the product.

DEFINITION OF DONE
The app is complete when a fresh developer can follow the README, create and migrate the database, run the app, sign in as each relevant role, complete every named journey using real persisted data, refresh without losing state, recover from common failures, and use the core interface on phone and desktop. All acceptance scenarios pass, permission boundaries are covered by tests, and no core screen is left as a placeholder.

NON-GOALS
Do not build visual automation journeys, a template marketplace, multivariate tests, or a full CRM.

Build just one piece

Not ready to replace all of Mailchimp? Fine. These are the peculiar sub-problems hiding inside it — the parts that are actually interesting to build. Each one is a standalone prompt, scoped to an evening, no strings attached to the full build.

Double opt-in that can’t be replayedSingle-use expiring confirmation tokens, with consent evidence you can point at.
Build the double opt-in flow for a newsletter list.

A signup creates a subscriber in pending state and emails a confirmation link containing a single-use token with an expiry (24 to 48 hours). Confirming flips the subscriber to active exactly once: a second click on the same link should show a friendly already-confirmed page, not an error and not a second state change. An expired token offers to resend a fresh one; resending invalidates all previous tokens for that address.

Record consent evidence at each step: signup source, signup timestamp, confirmation timestamp, and the form or import the address came from. Normalize emails (trim, lowercase) so [email protected] and [email protected] are one subscriber, and make a repeat signup for an already-active address a silent no-op rather than a duplicate row.

The hard rule: no campaign send may ever include a pending subscriber. Enforce it in the audience query, not in UI.

Done when: an unconfirmed address receives zero campaigns, a reused token changes nothing, and every active subscriber has a complete consent trail.
The send button that freezes everythingSnapshot content and recipients at schedule time, then deliver each email at most once.
Build the send pipeline for an email campaign, designed around one fear: sending someone the same newsletter twice.

When the owner schedules a campaign, freeze it: subject, body, links, the recipient query, and a concrete snapshot of the resolved recipient list. Edits after this point require cancelling and rescheduling — the report must describe exactly what was sent, not what the draft later became.

Create one delivery record per (campaign, subscriber) pair with a unique constraint, and use that pair as the idempotency key when talking to the email provider. The nasty case: the provider accepts a message but the connection times out, so your worker retries. With a stable idempotency key, the retry must not produce a second email. Track each delivery through queued, sent, failed, and retrying states, and let failed deliveries retry without touching successful ones.

Done when: a worker crash mid-send followed by a restart produces at most one email per subscriber, the report shows one final outcome per recipient, and post-schedule edits provably cannot alter what recipients receive.
Unsubscribe wins the raceSuppression checked at send time, not at schedule time.
Build the suppression layer for a newsletter sender — the machinery that guarantees an unsubscribe is honored even mid-send.

Maintain a suppression list fed by two sources: unsubscribe clicks and hard bounces reported by the email provider. The critical rule: check suppression immediately before each individual send attempt, not when the campaign was scheduled. If a subscriber unsubscribes while a 10,000-recipient campaign is grinding through its queue and their delivery has not been attempted yet, suppression wins and their delivery is marked suppressed, not sent.

Process provider bounce and complaint webhooks idempotently — providers retry, and a replayed hard-bounce event must not create duplicate suppression entries or double-count in reports. Distinguish hard bounces (suppress forever) from soft bounces (retry with a cap, then give up without suppressing). And make suppression sticky: a CSV import containing a suppressed address must never quietly resubscribe it.

Done when: an unsubscribe during an in-flight campaign prevents that email, replayed webhooks produce no duplicate suppressions, and no send path exists that skips the suppression check.
The CSV importer that trusts nothingDry-run mapping, duplicate detection, and formula escaping before a single row lands.
Build a subscriber CSV importer that assumes the file is hostile and the columns are wrong.

Start with a dry run: parse the file, let the user map columns to fields (email, tags, source), and show a full preview — how many rows are valid, which rows have malformed emails, which are duplicates within the file itself, and which collide with existing subscribers. Nothing is written until the user confirms the valid subset.

Handle CSV reality: quoted fields containing commas and newlines, BOM prefixes, mixed line endings, and duplicate header names. Normalize emails before deduplicating so casing differences collapse. For collisions with existing subscribers, merge only the fields the user explicitly selected — never overwrite consent data or resurrect a suppressed address.

On the way back out, escape spreadsheet formulas in exports: any cell starting with =, +, -, or @ gets prefixed so a malicious "subscriber name" cannot execute when the owner opens the export in Excel.

Done when: a dry run mutates nothing, a file with 3 bad rows imports the rest cleanly with per-row errors, and exported CSVs are formula-inert.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to build your own Mailchimp?

A basic version — subscriber list, send a broadcast — takes about 12 hours. Roughly 4 days gets you a solid v1 with double opt-in, tags, scheduling, open tracking. Matching everything Mailchimp really does (visual journeys, template builder, full crm) is closer to 3 months+, which is exactly why you should scope down instead.

How much does Mailchimp cost if I keep subscribing?

Mailchimp runs $20–$350 per month on public paid plans, which is $240–$4200 per year. A focused self-built replacement costs your build time plus close-to-zero hosting.

What stack should I use to build a Mailchimp alternative?

The build prompt on this page ships in four flavors: the AHA stack (Astro, HTMX, Alpine.js), Next.js, Laravel, and Ruby on Rails. The capability context for this product is Astro, D1, Workers. Pick the stack you already know — the scope matters more than the framework.

What features does a minimal Mailchimp replacement need?

A useful v1 needs: subscribe form with double opt-in and unsubscribe; subscriber table with tags, status, source, and CSV import; plain, markdown, or simple HTML campaign editor; test send, schedule, send progress, and failure retry; delivery, open, and click summary with privacy-aware tracking. Everything else is scope creep until you personally miss it.

What should I deliberately not build?

Do not build visual automation journeys, a template marketplace, multivariate tests, or a full CRM.

Does this build also replace MailerLite, Brevo, ActiveCampaign and Constant Contact?

Yes. Mailchimp, MailerLite, Brevo, ActiveCampaign and Constant Contact all solve the same core job — manage consented subscribers and send reliable, measurable broadcasts. The scoped v1 on this page covers what most people use any of them for, so one focused build replaces whichever you currently pay for.
